## Learning guide
**The arc**
Which Claude-based automation systems will businesses pay for, and how should you sell them? The central idea is to sell a business outcome rather than AI functionality, using four offers that address paperwork, lead follow-up, content production, and scattered company knowledge. The guide progresses from these workflows and their sales approaches to the final selection rule: diagnose whether the business needs more customers or more capacity before prescribing a system.

**1. Turn field voice notes into reviewed documents**
- **Automate familiar input:** Let field workers submit a WhatsApp voice note instead of typing paperwork after a day on site. Transcribe it, match it to the project's job record, and produce a branded PDF for documents such as site registers, risk assessments, and plant registers.
- **Keep human sign-off:** Save the PDF automatically to SharePoint and send it to the relevant recipients, but keep it as a draft until a person reviews and signs it off. This reduces administrative work without removing the review step.
- **Price the time and risk:** Ask how many hours foremen spend on paperwork each week and convert that time into a monetary figure. Five foremen spending five hours each means 25 paid hours per week; paperwork running days behind also creates safety and compliance risk.
- **Start with one disliked document:** Choose a non-desk-based trade such as construction, plumbing, or electrical work and ask which document the team most dislikes completing. Automate that one first, then expand once it works; familiar voice notes reduce the change required from workers.

**2. Connect interest to paid, booked appointments**
- **Build the complete follow-up path:** Generate weekly social posts in the owner's voice, then route interest-form submissions into the CRM. Send a text and email with a payment link; after payment, send intake forms and waivers with reminders until signed, followed by a booking link, Zoom confirmation, and appointment reminders.
- **Qualify the buyer:** Look for a booking-based business where leads arrive through forms or calls, receive little follow-up, and require manual chasing. This makes the offer about replacing an existing burden rather than introducing an unnecessary workflow.
- **Measure the conversion gap:** Compare form submissions with paying customers to frame the revenue opportunity. If 40 people submit a form and only 10 pay, there are 30 prospects to follow up with; connect the project fee to the value of additional paying clients.
- **Map before automating:** Put the path from first contact to booked call on one page and identify the steps that require a human. Automate the intervening steps, then record and send a short demonstration so the prospect can see the proposed workflow.

**3. Build a creator OS around an existing offer**
- **Target a production bottleneck:** Work with a creator or coach who already sells something and has a proven back-end offering but cannot keep up with content production. A creator OS can generate narrated videos and on-brand images using the creator's cloned voice and likeness, reducing the need to film or write everything manually.
- **Sell the outcome:** Connect increased content output to the buyer's actual product or service economics rather than leading with technical requirements. Use the chain more content → more attention → more leads → more sales, and calculate the sales needed to cover the fee: about 19 additional sales of a $197 guide cover a $3,600 build.
- **Demonstrate the voice:** Use 20 to 30 existing posts to build a voice profile, generate three pieces in that voice, and request feedback. This gives the prospect something concrete to judge before committing to a larger service.
- **Stage the commitment:** If the samples land well, offer to run one platform for one month before selling the full build and a monthly service. Tie the recurring fee to ongoing weekly content needs and whether the system is actually working.

**4. Make company knowledge usable across the team**
- **Start with recurring questions:** Choose one business type and identify the questions its team asks every week. Connect only the tools containing the necessary answers, such as Drive, Calendar, Gmail, Asana, or HubSpot, rather than connecting everything at once.
- **Recover search time:** Ask how long employees spend finding information or waiting for someone else to answer. A 20-person team losing two hours each per week represents 40 payroll hours; use that calculation and faster onboarding to explain the business value.
- **Build a reusable foundation:** Use the company brain personally first so you have a real demonstration and firsthand experience of its value. Reuse the foundation for clients, then add relevant workflows such as daily briefings, project recaps, meeting-transcript filing, or Claude Code routines once the necessary context is available.
- **Budget for adoption:** Treat change management as part of the cost, not an afterthought. Give the team time to build skills and shift toward consulting the system first instead of searching scattered documents, Slack channels, or colleagues' memories.
- **Choose a delivery model:** Decide whether to emphasize higher-ticket, template-based delivery or lower-ticket, custom work. The commercial examples contrast a $15,000 reusable-foundation offer with $2,000 custom systems; additions can include role-based dashboards, document generators, and research briefs for new leads.

**5. Select the offer by the business constraint**
- **Experiment before specializing:** Try systems, gather data, and gain delivery experience before narrowing your offer. Become good at one system and find businesses that need it, rather than choosing a niche solely because you feel pressure to specialize.
- **Diagnose demand versus capacity:** If the business needs more customers, consider the content system or intake funnel. If it already has more work than it can handle, prioritize the voice-note system or company brain to return time; more leads would add pressure without resolving the bottleneck.
- **Make the business case explicit:** Position yourself as a business-growth provider who uses AI when it fits. Give the buyer a strong reason to spend money and accept risk, accounting for time, change management, and proof of results rather than merely presenting a productized solution.

**Try this first:** Ask one business owner whether their main constraint is too few customers or too little capacity to handle existing work, then use that answer to select which workflow to explore.

**Caveat:** The reported prices, client outcomes, demand, and company-brain accuracy are not independently verified, and increased content does not establish increased sales. These notes teach positioning and workflow design, not detailed technical implementation.
